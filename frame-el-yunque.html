Cloud Water

El Yunque National Forest, Luquillo Mountains, Puerto Rico

El Yunque sits in the Luquillo Mountains at the northeast corner of Puerto Rico, less than an hour's drive from San Juan. It is the only tropical rainforest in the United States National Forest System. The trade winds come in off the Atlantic heavy with moisture, hit the mountains, and rise; the air cools and lets go of its water. On the high peaks that can mean well over a hundred inches of rain a year, and on many days the summits disappear into cloud.

All that water has to go somewhere, and it goes downhill in streams like this one, sliding over boulders worn smooth and furred with moss. The plants along the banks tell you where you are. The fan-topped trees overhead are sierra palms, which crowd the wettest slopes. The lacy crowns are tree ferns, a lineage far older than the flowering trees around them, and the strap-leaved plants growing up the trunk at the right are bromeliads, rooted in the air rather than the soil.

The forest has been protected longer than most. The Taíno people, who lived on the island before the Spanish arrived, held these mountains sacred, and the name El Yunque is often traced to Yukiyu, a benevolent spirit believed to dwell on the peaks. Spain's crown set part of the mountains aside as a reserve in 1876. After the island passed to the United States, President Theodore Roosevelt proclaimed the Luquillo Forest Reserve in 1903, making it one of the oldest reserves in the Western Hemisphere. It later became the Caribbean National Forest and took its present name in 2007.

The trade winds carry the ocean up the mountain, and the streams carry it back down.

Night belongs to the coquí, a tree frog no bigger than a thumbnail whose two-note call, co-kee, is the sound of Puerto Rico after dark. The forest is also the last wild stronghold of the Puerto Rican parrot, once reduced to barely a dozen birds in the 1970s and slowly coaxed back through captive breeding.

In September 2017, Hurricane María crossed the island and stripped much of El Yunque bare, snapping trees and closing trails for months. A rainforest recovers on its own schedule, and this one has been growing back ever since: the palms and ferns first, then the canopy, closing over streams like this one again.
