The Long Reach

Michael at the Skee-Ball lanes, Midway Park, Maple Springs, New York

Every child who has ever played Skee-Ball has tried this. The rules say to roll from the end of the lane. Michael had other ideas. He planted one knee, stretched his whole body up the ramp until his sneakers barely touched the floor, and let the ball go from as close to the rings as his arm would carry him. I stood behind him with the camera and didn't say a word.

The lanes he was cheating on have their own long history. Skee-Ball was patented in 1908 by Joseph Fourestier Simpson of Vineland, New Jersey, and the first alleys were enormous, more than thirty feet long. It took a Philadelphia businessman, J. D. Este, to make it a sensation, installing lanes on the Atlantic City boardwalk in 1914. The machines shrank over the decades to fit arcades like this one, but the idea never changed: roll a wooden ball up a ramp, hop it over the bump, and hope it drops in the small ring at the top.

Midway is older than the game. It opened on July 12, 1898, a picnic ground built by the Jamestown & Lake Erie Railway to give people a reason to ride the trolley on weekends. The name came from a steamboat dock built a few years earlier, halfway between the landings at Point Whiteside and Maple Springs. A pavilion went up in 1915 with a dining room and a floor for dancing and roller skating, and the rides that came later, many built by the Allan Herschell Company just up the road in North Tonawanda, are still turning today.

The rules say to roll from the end of the lane. Michael had other ideas.

When this photograph was taken, Midway was still a family business. Martin "Red" Walsh had bought the park in 1951, and the next generation of Walshes took over in 1984. A few years after this picture, the Walshes sold it to New York State, and it reopened as Midway State Park in 2007, the only amusement park in the entire state park system. It is one of just a dozen trolley parks left in the country.

For families around Chautauqua Lake, it has always been the place you go when the kids are small. Tokens in a cup, a strip of tickets curling out of the machine, the clatter of balls rolling back down the return chute. I don't remember the score that day, or what he traded his tickets for. I remember the reach.
