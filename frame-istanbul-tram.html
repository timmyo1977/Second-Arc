İstiklal Avenue, Beyoğlu, Istanbul

İstiklal Avenue runs for less than a mile through the old European quarter of Istanbul, from Taksim Square down toward the Golden Horn. Under the Ottomans it was the Grande Rue de Péra, the street of the foreign embassies, where diplomats, merchants, and travelers from across Europe and the Levant mixed in its cafés, arcades, and theaters. When the Turkish Republic was proclaimed in 1923, the street was renamed İstiklal, which means Independence, after the war that created the new nation.

Trams came to Istanbul in 1871, first pulled by horses, and the city's lines were later electrified. For decades they rattled up and down this avenue, until the last trams on the European side were retired around 1961 and the city gave itself over to buses and cars. At the bottom of the street, the Tünel, an underground funicular opened in 1875, still climbs the hill from the waterfront. It is the second-oldest underground railway in the world, after London's.

The tram came back in 1990, when the avenue was closed to traffic and turned into a pedestrian street. The city restored original cars from the old network, and the little red line now runs about a mile with five stops, Taksim at one end and Tünel at the other, exactly as the sign on its roof says. The car in this photograph is number 410, one of those restored originals. Painted on its front, in Turkish, is a warning: hanging on is forbidden and dangerous.

An old red tram, its single headlamp lit, moving at a walking pace through a crowd that barely bothers to step aside.

On any evening, the avenue fills with people walking: shoppers, students, families, couples, and visitors, past bookshops, cinemas, churches, and old embassy buildings. The tram does not hurry. It rings its bell and rolls slowly through the crowd, the way the trams did when the street still had a French name.
