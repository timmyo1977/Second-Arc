Counsel at Law

Kaitlin, J.D.

Years after the crimson and purple regalia of commencement day, the J.D. has been put thoroughly to work. Kaitlin is a litigator now, representing clients in complex commercial disputes — the kind of document-heavy cases where the answer lives three layers down in a clause nobody read carefully enough.

She has been recognized by her peers as one of the rising lawyers in her market — a recognition that the people practicing alongside her already saw coming.

Practice & Pursuits

The Work

Complex Commercial Disputes

The bread and butter of a commercial litigator's docket: contract disputes, business torts, disagreements between parties that were, at some prior and more optimistic moment, supposed to be working together. Preparing complaints and motions, reviewing records, and — in the cases that don't need a jury — negotiating settlements that let everyone move on.

Health Care Matters

Some of her most delicate work involves health care institutions and the families of patients who can no longer manage their own affairs. These cases almost always involve a family that is grieving something even when nobody is dying. Good counsel here is more than good lawyering.

Estates, Employment & Agencies

A rounded commercial practice also touches estate disputes, employment claims, and challenges to administrative decisions — the moments when the machinery gets something wrong and someone needs to push back through the proper channels.

Research & Writing

In law school she worked on research supporting a brief filed with the United States Supreme Court and taught legal writing to other students — the sort of preparation that makes a young attorney's first appellate brief feel like familiar terrain.

A Father's Note

The Long View

"A father's job, in the best version of the story, is to be present at these moments — not as the protagonist, but as a witness."

There is a version of this story where the law school graduation is the end of the arc — the robes, the ceremony, the photograph on the chapel steps, and then whatever comes next is off camera. But the interesting part of any commencement is precisely what it says on the tin: this is a beginning. The degree is the ticket to the arena, not the trophy at the end of it.

Years on, the ticket has been used. Kaitlin has built an actual practice — a docket, a reputation among the judges and clerks she appears before, clients who call her because they have been told she knows what she is doing, opposing counsel who have learned to prepare carefully for her filings. She has moved from a first-job firm to a major regional practice, and then up. This is not a credential. It is a career.

Litigation is one of the harder ways to make a living as a lawyer. The hours are unpredictable, the adversarial posture is wearing, and the cases that matter most are rarely the ones that resolve cleanly. It takes a particular kind of mind to enjoy the work — a tolerance for ambiguity, a gift for reading a statute and finding the seam, a willingness to argue a position in the morning and spot its weakness by lunch. Kaitlin has always had some version of that mind. Law school merely handed it a vocabulary.

Recognition from one's own professional community is the kind of honor that looks, at first glance, like politeness — a nod to a younger attorney on the way up. It isn't. The lawyers who vote on these things have actually seen the work, in court and across the table. When they pick someone, it means something.

There is a good life, a full table of friends, and a career that is now fully her own. A father cannot claim credit for any of it. The best he can do is notice, carefully and in full, what has been built.
