Peacetime

Utah Beach, Sainte-Marie-du-Mont, Normandy

Before dawn on June 6, 1944, this stretch of sand was the far western edge of the largest seaborne invasion in history. Utah was the westernmost of the five D-Day beaches, at the base of the Cotentin Peninsula, and it belonged to the American 4th Infantry Division. Paratroopers of the 82nd and 101st Airborne had dropped into the darkness inland hours earlier. At 6:30 a.m., the first landing craft reached the shore.

They reached the wrong shore. With the shoreline hidden in smoke, strong currents had carried the first wave nearly two thousand yards south of its target. Brigadier General Theodore Roosevelt Jr., the son of a president, walked the beach with a cane. At fifty-six he was the oldest man in the assault, and he saw that the new ground was more lightly defended. Rather than reorganize under fire, he made the decision that saved the morning: we'll start the war from right here. The follow-on waves were sent to him. He would earn the Medal of Honor for that day and die of a heart attack five weeks later. He is buried farther east along the coast, in the American cemetery above Omaha Beach.

The landings were timed for low tide so the engineers could see and clear the German obstacles. That is why the beach in this photograph runs so far out to the water. By nightfall the 4th Division had put some twenty-one thousand men ashore, at a cost of fewer than two hundred casualties. A few miles down the coast at Omaha, the toll ran into the thousands. Behind the dunes, the Germans had flooded the fields, and the only ways inland were narrow causeways across the water.

Sixty-eight years later, the same sand belongs to a row of boys on a seawall, one of them fast asleep in the sun.

Today the dunes hold monuments to the units that came ashore. The Utah Beach Landing Museum stands on the site of a German strongpoint. It was founded in 1962 by the mayor of Sainte-Marie-du-Mont, who borrowed money to build it. Families still walk the flats at low tide, kids still dig in the sand, and the wind still comes in off the Channel. This photograph doesn't mention the war, and it doesn't need to. What it shows is what those men crossed the water for: an ordinary summer afternoon, with nothing to fear on the beach at all.
