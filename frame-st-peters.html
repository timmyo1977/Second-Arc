Open Arms

St. Peter's Square, seen from Michelangelo's dome, Vatican City

This is the view you earn. The lantern atop Michelangelo's dome is reached by a climb of more than five hundred steps, the last stretch up a narrow stair that leans with the curve of the dome itself. At the top you step out onto a ring of open air above the largest church in Christendom, and the whole of Rome lies at your feet.

Directly below is the square Gian Lorenzo Bernini designed for Pope Alexander VII between 1656 and 1667. Its two great colonnades, four columns deep, curve out from the basilica like arms. Bernini said as much: they were meant to be the arms of the Church, reaching out to embrace the faithful. Along their rooftops stand some 140 statues of saints, and in the foreground of this photograph are the figures that crown the basilica's façade, Christ with John the Baptist and the apostles, looking out over the same view.

The obelisk at the center is older than Christianity. It was quarried in Egypt and brought to Rome by the emperor Caligula to decorate his circus, which stood just beside this spot, and where tradition holds that Saint Peter was crucified. In 1586 Pope Sixtus V had it moved to the middle of the square, an engineering feat by Domenico Fontana that took hundreds of men, scores of horses and months of planning. It is the only obelisk in Rome never to have fallen.

Bernini built the colonnades as arms, and from the dome you can see the embrace.

The broad avenue running straight toward the river is younger than it looks. Via della Conciliazione was cut through the old medieval Borgo after the 1929 Lateran Treaty settled relations between Italy and the Holy See, opening the long axis you see here. At its end, beside the Tiber, sits the round bulk of Castel Sant'Angelo, built as the emperor Hadrian's tomb and later turned into a papal fortress. Far off to the right, the white marble Vittoriano marks the center of the city.

Few places pack so much history into a single glance: an Egyptian obelisk, a Roman emperor's tomb, a Renaissance dome, a Baroque square and a twentieth-century boulevard, all arranged along one line from the river to the altar. When this photograph was taken, in late March 2013, Pope Francis had been elected just two weeks earlier, and the rows of chairs below had been set out for his first Holy Week.
