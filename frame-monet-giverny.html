The Water Garden

Claude Monet's Garden, Giverny, Normandy

Claude Monet came to Giverny in 1883, renting a pink farmhouse in a village on the Seine about fifty miles downriver from Paris. In 1890, once his paintings had finally begun to sell, he bought it. Then he began to garden the way he painted, in color and light, planting beds of flowers chosen for how they would look together rather than for any botanical order.

In 1893 he bought the marshy meadow across the road and set out to build something new: a pond. He needed the authorities' permission to divert water from a small branch of the river Epte, and his neighbors worried that his exotic plants would poison their water. He got the permission anyway. He planted new hybrid water lilies from a nursery in the southwest of France, built an arched Japanese footbridge, and hung the banks with willows, irises, and climbing roses. He employed gardeners to tend it, one of whom rowed out each morning to wash the dust from the lily pads.

Then he painted it, over and over, for the rest of his life. The water lilies became several hundred canvases. After the Armistice in 1918, he promised the French nation a cycle of huge panoramic panels, which were installed in two oval rooms at the Orangerie in Paris in 1927, the year after his death.

He planted the garden so that he could paint it, and then painted it for the rest of his life.

Those late paintings were made through clouded eyes. Monet's doctors had diagnosed cataracts in both eyes years earlier, and by the early 1920s reds had turned muddy to him and pinks looked flat. In 1923, at eighty-two, he finally agreed to surgery. Afterward he destroyed some of the canvases he had painted half-blind, because their colors now looked wrong, and went back to work with blues and greens.

After his death the garden fell into ruin. His son Michel left the property to the Académie des Beaux-Arts in 1966, and with French and American donors it was restored and opened to the public in 1980. Today it is planted as he kept it. In June, when this photograph was taken, the astilbe is in feathery bloom along the paths, the roses hang over the water, and the lilies are opening on the pond.
